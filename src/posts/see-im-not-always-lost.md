While most people know GeoGuessr as a digital game built on fast geographical deduction, I recently used it in a very different context: reminiscence sessions for people affected by dementia in a South London Memory Café.

As a Senior Product Designer focused on digital inclusion, I've spent the last year running remote Memory Cafés with AgeUK. These sessions support people affected by dementia and their carers, aiming to stimulate memory, cognition, and social connection. Technology is never the focus for its own sake. Sometimes success is as simple as learning one new thing or identifying dangers.

Traditional sessions typically centre on the careful act of remembering. But memory-on-demand, particularly when affected by dementia, cannot be forced. Standard tools often include [memory boxes](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/reminiscence-and-reminiscence-therapy): curated collections of objects like a bus ticket or a scrap of fabric. These are tactile and storied, but limited, and difficult to translate to a shared remote setting.

This session tested moving from object-based remembering to something spatial and dynamic, using digital maps in the form of Google Maps and GeoGuessr. Reducing the pressure of remembering, and offering a dynamic interface, decreased cognitive stress, increased interaction, and let memories surface naturally.

## Digital maps as tools for remembering

I was wary of introducing GeoGuessr immediately. Dropping participants into a random stretch of road in rural America or Southeast Asia risked feeling abstract and impersonal. We began closer to home, in South London.

Using Google Maps, we navigated familiar streets together. I prompted simple questions: How would you get there? What used to stand here? Participants shared stories, grounding the digital landscape in personal narratives. The workshop quickly took on a life of its own. Visiting present-day locations sparked discussions about layers of local history. Unlike static prompts, the digital map let us follow curiosity in real time, zooming in and taking unplanned detours.

One participant, whose dementia is the most progressed in the group, navigated me toward an independent record shop, Wanted Music. "Turn right, past the bicycle shop, past the tree," he directed. Step by step, he reconstructed the route from memory. "A little bit further!" he exclaimed.

When we arrived, he said: "See! I'm not always lost." The group laughed. He explained that he used to visit the shop after annual hospital appointments for diabetic retinopathy. The treatment required him to avoid bright sunlight shortly after, so he would head into the dimly lit record shop across the street from the hospital for some refuge, often leaving with five or ten CDs.

## From reminiscence to deduction

Only after grounding ourselves locally did I introduce GeoGuessr, thinking carefully about inclusive design for older audiences to reduce anxiety around unfamiliar technology. At first, the guesses were tentative: a forested road in Bangladesh, a barren American highway.

Soon, the group began paying attention to vegetation, road width, driving side, architecture, and language on signs. They were exercising cognitive skills through observation and reasoning rather than direct recall. One participant fetched a magnifying glass to inspect the screen. We zoomed in on a hand-painted sign in the Philippines: "Success doesn't come to you, you come to it." This felt apt. Progress was emerging through curiosity and shared discovery.

For participants living with dementia, this visual and spatial problem-solving offered a way to engage the brain. The randomness of the game revealed how easily we become fixed in the familiar. The unfamiliar did not alienate the group; it broadened their perspectives. For a group often defined by perceived loss, this felt meaningful. Curiosity, deduction, and exploration proved just as important as the act of remembering itself.

## A broader view of inclusion in product design

For older adults, even those living with cognitive decline, the desire for exploration does not disappear with age. As an industry, we often default to a "subtractive" model of inclusive design. We assume accessibility means stripped-back interfaces, fewer choices, and "bigger buttons." Clarity is essential, but over-simplification can slide into infantilisation.

Inclusive design should move beyond the compliance checklist. If we only solve for "ease of use," we risk designing out the very things that make an experience worth having: curiosity, deduction, and the thrill of discovery. Cognitive engagement is not the enemy of inclusion.

For designers working in health, social care, or any digital space, here are some key takeaways:

- **Reframing over rebuilding.** Impact often lies in reframing existing, "unremarkable" tools to create new conditions for connection, rather than building siloed "senior" tech.
- **User-led exploration.** True inclusion is about preserving dignity. Shift the UX from a test of memory or ability to a collaborative exploration where the user leads.
- **Design for the healthy challenge.** Dignity lives in participation. We shouldn't discredit the digital appetite of older audiences.

At a time when the design industry is preoccupied with AI, speed, and optimisation, this is a necessary counterpoint: technology as an enabler of connection for vulnerable groups.
